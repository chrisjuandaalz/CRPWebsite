When "Too Healthy" Becomes a Warning Sign: The Case Against Chasing RBC Above 700%

By Christian Juanda • September 7, 2026

The Assumption Nobody Questions

If 120% is the regulatory floor and 300% is "very healthy," conventional wisdom says the arithmetic should continue in a straight line: higher is better, and a company sitting at 700%, 900%, or beyond must simply be the safest choice of all.

This is where critical thinking has to push back. In capital management, there is a point where "very healthy" quietly becomes "something doesn't add up." An RBC ratio that looks like a fortress can just as easily be evidence of a company that doesn't know what to do with its own balance sheet — or is choosing not to.

Capital Has a Job. Idle Capital Isn't Doing It.

Capital held against risk is meant to be working capital — sized to the risks the company actually underwrites, invested to generate returns, and cycled back into better pricing, product innovation, and service for policyholders. An RBC of 300-500% signals a company that has calibrated its capital to a real risk appetite: aggressive enough to write meaningful business and compete on price, conservative enough to comfortably absorb shocks.

An RBC of 700%+ raises a different question entirely: why is so much capital sitting idle instead of being deployed? In corporate finance, excess capital that isn't reinvested, returned, or actively working is typically a symptom of one of a few things, none of which are flattering:

  • Capital inefficiency — the company doesn't have a compelling growth strategy, so capital piles up instead of being deployed into better products or expansion
  • Extreme underwriting conservatism — the company prices policies so cautiously (high premiums, narrow risk acceptance, restrictive underwriting) that it is effectively avoiding risk rather than managing it, often at the policyholder's expense in the form of overpriced premiums
  • Low business volume — a smaller or stagnant book of business relative to a large capital base will mechanically produce a sky-high ratio, not because the company is exceptionally strong, but because the denominator (required capital) is small
  • Shareholder-first capital hoarding — capital retained for future dividends, M&A war chests, or parent-company objectives rather than policyholder value

None of these are the story a marketing brochure tells when it prints "RBC over 700%" in bold. But they are the more statistically likely explanations.

The Efficiency Argument: What 300-500% Actually Signals

A company sustaining RBC in the 300-500% range over multiple years — not dipping toward the 120% floor, but not ballooning past 700% either — is telling you something more useful than "we have a lot of money." It's telling you:

  1. The company is actively underwriting — it's taking on real risk at real volume, which is the entire point of an insurance company existing
  2. Capital is being allocated with discipline — enough buffer to be safe, not so much that it's sitting unused
  3. Pricing is likely more competitive — capital that isn't being hoarded doesn't need to be recovered through inflated premiums
  4. Management is making active decisions — rather than defaulting to maximum conservatism, the company is managing risk-adjusted returns

This is the difference between a fortress and a factory. A fortress just sits there, impressive and unproductive. A factory takes in risk, processes it efficiently, and produces value — for shareholders and policyholders alike.

The Question an Ultra-High RBC Should Actually Prompt

Instead of treating 700%+ as an automatic green light, critical due diligence asks:

What to Investigate Why It Matters
Is the high RBC due to genuinely low liabilities (small, low-risk book), or genuinely high capital reserves? A tiny denominator inflates the ratio without reflecting real strength
Has the ratio been climbing for multiple years without deployment into growth or product improvement? Suggests capital stagnation, not strategic strength
How does the company's premium pricing compare to competitors at 300-500% RBC? Ultra-conservative capital positioning is often recovered through higher premiums
What is management doing with the surplus — reinvestment, dividends, or nothing? Reveals whether the "strength" benefits policyholders or just the balance sheet
Is this a smaller, newer player with a thin book of business? Explains inflated ratios that have nothing to do with resilience

The Closing Argument

Solvency is not a competition to maximize. It is a target to meet responsibly — enough buffer to survive genuine stress, not so much that capital sits frozen instead of financing better products, better pricing, and better service.

A company at 300-500% RBC has demonstrated it can be both safe and active. A company at 700%+ has demonstrated, at minimum, that it has questions to answer about why its capital isn't working harder — and at worst, that its impressive number is a byproduct of a business that isn't writing enough real risk to matter.

The healthiest-looking number on the page is not always the healthiest company behind it. Ask what the capital is doing — not just how much of it there is.
