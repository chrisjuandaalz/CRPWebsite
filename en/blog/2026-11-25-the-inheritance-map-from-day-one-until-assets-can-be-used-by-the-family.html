The Inheritance Map: From Day One Until Assets Can Be Used by the Family

By Christian Juanda • November 25, 2026

Image is for illustration only.

Inheritance that isn't prepared doesn't disappear. It gets stuck: the assets are there, but the family cannot use them at the moment they need cash most.

This is the opening article of a seven-part series on the inheritance process for business owners and family businesses in Indonesia. Its purpose is education; there is no sales pitch. After reading this map, you will know the order a family goes through and which article to read first.

I am Christian Juanda, Insurance Strategist for Business Owners & Family Businesses. I have been in the insurance industry since 2017 and have served 500+ clients, among them family business owners and high-net-worth individuals. This series is not legal or tax advice: inheritance rules differ by population group, religion, and region, so confirm the legal parts with your notary and tax consultant.

Illustration: assets exist, cash does not

The story below is a composite illustration of patterns I often see. The names and figures are examples, not one client's story.

Pak Hendra, 58, owns a building-materials distribution company with 40 employees. He holds 70% of the shares and is also the president director. His family consists of his wife, two children who already work in the company, and one child still at university. His assets: a house, a shop-house, the company shares, a time deposit, and a personal bank account.

One morning Pak Hendra passes away suddenly. A week later payroll falls due, suppliers demand payment, and the bank asks about a credit facility that he had personally guaranteed. The family tries to withdraw the deposit and to sign a company cheque, and discovers that none of the assets can be used that very day. The cash needed for the first three months in this illustration is around Rp1.2 billion.

Why I call it “trapped inheritance”

For more than ten years I worked in IT, most recently as an SAP analyst handling inventory, purchasing, and accounts payable systems. There I learned one thing: inventory is capital trapped in the form of goods. A full warehouse does not let a company pay salaries. Inheritance works the same way.

From the patterns I see, assets are usually trapped by three things:

  1. Documents. It is unclear who the legitimate heirs are, whether there is a will, and where the ownership documents are kept.
  2. Authority. It is unclear who may decide and sign when the owner is gone.
  3. Cash. All the wealth sits in assets that take time to transfer or sell.

The seven articles in this series follow these three things one by one, in the real order a family experiences them.
